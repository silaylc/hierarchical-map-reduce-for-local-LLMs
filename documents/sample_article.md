# Heat Pumps in Cold Climates (sample document)

## Background

Air-source heat pumps move heat from outdoor air into a building instead of
generating it by burning fuel. Their efficiency is expressed as the
coefficient of performance (COP): a COP of 3 means three units of heat are
delivered for every unit of electricity consumed.

## Performance at low temperatures

Efficiency falls as the outdoor temperature drops, because the pump has to
lift heat across a larger temperature difference. Older units often fell
below a COP of 1.5 at -15 °C. Newer cold-climate models using variable-speed
compressors and vapour injection keep a COP of around 2 at the same
temperature.

## Sizing and backup heat

Undersized units run continuously on the coldest days and may need electric
resistance backup, which has a COP of exactly 1. Sizing for the local design
temperature reduces how often the backup runs.

## Risks

- Defrost cycles temporarily reduce output on humid, near-freezing days.
- Grid demand peaks when many homes heat at the same time.
- Installation quality strongly affects real-world efficiency.

## Conclusion

Cold-climate heat pumps are a practical option where units are sized for the
local design temperature and installed carefully; backup heat should be
planned for rare extreme days rather than avoided entirely.
